## Executive Summary

#### Project Context
The Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries Wealth and Water Resources (MAFWR) of the Sultanate of Oman has commissioned Renardet S.A. & Partners Consulting Engineers L.L.C. in joint venture with Artelia Muscat to undertake a comprehensive feasibility study and masterplan design for a Flood Protection Scheme in North and South Al Batinah Governorates. This report documents the hydraulic design of flood protection and stormwater channels for Block 6 of the project area.

#### Scope of Work
Block 6 encompasses the coastal plain and wadi systems draining towards the Arabian Sea. A total of 252 channel reaches were hydraulically designed, covering Flood Protection (FP) channels, Stormwater Main (Sw M) channels, and Stormwater Secondary (Sw S) channels. The design target is the 200-year return period flood event. All channels are sized with upstream dams in place, as these structures play a critical role in attenuating peak inflows before they enter the channel network.

#### Hydrological Approach
Design rainfall depths and temporal distribution patterns are derived from the nationwide hydrology study conducted by MAFWR, based on Regional Frequency Analysis (RFA) of all available rainfall station records. The Probable Maximum Precipitation (PMP) is estimated using the storm transposition and moisture maximisation method (WMO). Area Reduction Factors (ARF) are applied to point rainfall to account for spatial variability over larger catchments. Rainfall-runoff simulation is performed in HEC-HMS using the SCS Curve Number method, with Muskingum–Cunge reach routing between sub-catchments and dam sites. Reservoir routing accounts for dam attenuation in reducing the 200-year peak flows to the channel network.

#### Hydraulic Design Methodology
Channel sizing is performed using Manning’s steady-state flow equation implemented in SewerGEMS. Channels are designed with trapezoidal cross-sections. The key design criteria include: channel depth of 2.5 m, 3 m, or 4 m depending on existing wadi depth; freeboard of 0.6 m; maximum allowable velocity of 3.0 m/s; Manning’s n of 0.03 for earth channels and 0.02 for concrete; minimum slope of 0.0003 m/m in flat coastal areas; and outfall bank heights determined from Mean Higher High Water (MHHW) levels from the Oman Maritime Book 2024. Channels are preferably designed in the cut condition to allow lateral inflow from surrounding areas.

#### Key Findings
The total designed channel network in Block 6 comprises 295 km of channels: 104 km of Flood Protection channels, 103 km of Stormwater Main channels, and 88 km of Stormwater Secondary channels. Channel widths range from 5 m in upper headwater reaches to over 754 m in the main wadi trunks. The majority of channels (approximately 70%) have widths in the 50–200 m range. Several design constraints were encountered, including undersized road crossings at major highways, conflicts with PDO and OQ pipeline infrastructure, and progressive wadi corridor encroachment. In areas where the 200-year protection target cannot be achieved due to these physical constraints, protection is limited to the maximum feasible level.

#### Conclusions and Recommendations
The integrated flood protection system of upstream dams and designed channels provides a comprehensive level of protection against the 200-year flood event for the built-up areas of Block 6. The presence of dams in the masterplan is essential: without dam attenuation, channel widths in the main wadis would need to be substantially larger, increasing land acquisition, cost, and construction complexity. It is recommended that wadi corridors be formally reserved against future encroachment, that maintenance access routes be incorporated into the channel design, and that a coordinated approach to road crossing upgrades be pursued with the relevant authorities to enable full 200-year protection across all reaches.

The Probable Maximum Precipitation (PMP) is estimated using the storm transposition and moisture maximisation method as recommended by the World Meteorological Organisation (WMO). This approach involves identifying the most severe historical storm events in the region, transposing them to the catchment of interest, and maximising the precipitable water content to its theoretical upper limit based on atmospheric thermodynamic principles. The resulting PMP values represent the greatest depth of precipitation that is physically possible for a given duration and area, providing the basis for the design of critical hydraulic infrastructure. In this study, the nationwide PMP analysis conducted by MAFWR provides the 24-hour design rainfall depths used as input to the rainfall–runoff model. The dam spillways are sized based on probable maximum flood (PMF).

The Area Reduction Factor (ARF) accounts for the spatial variability of rainfall over a catchment. Point rainfall measurements tend to overestimate the average areal rainfall depth for large catchments, because intense storm cells rarely cover the entire catchment simultaneously. The ARF, a dimensionless factor ≤ 1, is applied to design point rainfall to obtain the spatially averaged areal rainfall. In this project, ARF values are derived from the nationwide hydrology study and applied to all catchments. This is especially significant for larger drainage areas in Block 6, where the ARF meaningfully reduces computed peak discharges.

The Curve Number (CN) grid was developed through the integration of two high-resolution global datasets: the ESA WorldCover 2020 (10 m) and the ISRIC SoilGrids (250 m). The ESA WorldCover product provides a refined classification of land use and land cover (LULC), allowing for a precise assessment of surface roughness and vegetation intercept. To account for subterranean influences on runoff, SoilGrids data was processed to determine Hydrologic Soil Groups (HSG) based on soil texture and drainage properties. The datasets were spatially aligned and combined via a standardized SCS (Soil Conservation Service) lookup table to generate a spatially distributed CN layer representative of the study area’s hydrologic response.

Reach routing between sub-catchments and dam sites is performed using the Muskingum–Cunge method, a physically based, variable-parameter routing technique that accounts for both translation and attenuation of the flood wave. The method assumes a trapezoidal channel cross-section and solves the kinematic wave equation with diffusion correction, using channel geometry (width, side slopes, Manning’s n, and bed slope) together with the input hydrograph to compute the outflow at each reach outlet. Unlike the simpler Muskingum method, Muskingum–Cunge derives its routing coefficients directly from channel hydraulics, eliminating the need for calibration. This makes it particularly suited to ungauged catchments and provides a physically realistic representation of flood wave propagation along the channel network.

### Outfalls
The outfall locations represent the most critical geometric constraints in the channel design. A fundamental requirement is that the outfall invert level must not fall below existing ground level, although a tolerance of up to 0.5 m below ground is considered acceptable in practice.
For three outfalls - O2 (Sw S), O4 (Sw S), and O10 (Sw M, Barka) - gravity drainage cannot be achieved within this tolerance. Since O2 and O4 serve Stormwater Secondary channels carrying relatively low flows, this limitation has minimal hydraulic consequence and is not considered critical.
O10 (Barka), however, presents the most significant challenge. A low-lying area approximately 1.65 km upstream of the outfall has a ground elevation of only +0.13 m (amsl), which provides insufficient head to maintain a positive bed slope toward the sea. Achieving gravity drainage would require a channel invert of -0.37 m (amsl) at the outfall - below mean sea level. At this depression point, the 200-year peak discharge reaches 986 m³/s, necessitating a channel width of approximately 624 m. Such a cross-section is geometrically and socially infeasible, as it would require extensive land acquisition and the relocation of existing residents.
Two alternative solutions are under consideration:
Pumping station: A dedicated flood pumping station sized for the design discharge to drain ponded water during flood events.
Pumping station with detention pond: A retention pond upstream of the pumping station stores and attenuates the flood volume, reducing the peak discharge that the pumps must handle. This significantly lowers the required pump capacity and capital cost, at the expense of the land required for the pond footprint.
At O19, the outfall of the diversion channel to Bani Kharous UP dam, the constraint is more tractable. Replacing the wide channel cross-section with a one-sided dyke arrangement allows the required conveyance capacity to be achieved within the available geometric corridor.

### Conclusion
The hydraulic design of flood protection and stormwater channels for Block 6 has been completed in accordance with the established design criteria and applicable technical standards. The integrated system of upstream dams, channel networks, and dykes provides a comprehensive and spatially coherent flood protection scheme for North and South Al Batinah Governorates.
Thanks to the strategic placement of dams in the masterplan, the required channel sizes in the main wadis are significantly reduced compared to an uncontrolled scenario. The dams attenuate the 200-year peak inflows to levels that can be accommodated by economically feasible channel cross-sections, thereby reducing land acquisition requirements and construction costs. At the downstream of proposed and upgraded dams, preservation of the existing wadi corridor is recommended, particularly to convey flows in excess of the design flood.
The total length of channels designed in Block 6 is 295 km, comprising 104 km of Flood Protection (FP) channels, 103 km of Stormwater Main (Sw M) channels, and 88 km of Stormwater Secondary (Sw S) channels. Across the 252 designed reaches, widths range from 5 m in upper headwater reaches at the downstream of dams to over 754 m in the outfalls. The majority of channels are designed in the cut condition, allowing lateral inflows from surrounding areas and reducing the need for dykes along channel banks. Additionally, downstream of dam the required width of channels is much less than existing wadi width. In this case it is advised to preserve and rehabilitate the existing wadi width to make sure in larger flow magnitudes, the downstream network will not overwhelm.
Several key challenges were identified and addressed during design. These include undersized road crossings at major highways (Batinah Expressway, Batinah Highway, and Batinah Coastal Road), which constrain channel hydraulic capacity at critical locations; conflicts with PDO and OQ pipelines at shallow burial depths along several wadi alignments; and progressive land-use encroachment that has narrowed natural wadi corridors. Additionally, the bottleneck in front of University of Technology and Applied Sciences Rustaq, causes huge backwater, which requires widening.
The outfall O10 at Barka marks the critical situation at which conveying the flow by gravity is not possible unless with wide channel ending at levels below existing culvert constraint and mean sea level, or implementing pumping station.
In road crossings, a total of 61 cross drainage structures are identified, out of which at 20 locations, there are no structures currently. And among others, only two bridges are sufficient to pass the design flood. The rest require an upgrade.
The dykes’ height is identified from the results of the 2D HEC-RAS models. There are a total of 50 dykes and their height vary between 1.1 m in plains and 4 m in Rustaq. The total length of dykes in block 06 is 87.5 km.
The cost of channels, dykes and culverts are 310.6, 15.3 and 37.8 million OMR respectively. The total cost of the three infrastructures is 363.7 million OMR.
The long-term effectiveness of the flood protection scheme depends not only on the physical infrastructure but also on institutional measures: formal reservation of wadi corridors against encroachment, regular maintenance of channel capacity, and enforcement of construction setback requirements along designated flood channels. The hydraulic designs presented in this report form the technical basis for the detailed engineering design, procurement, and construction phases.
